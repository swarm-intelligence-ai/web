Choosing a data platform that outlasts the roadmap

Most platform decisions are really decisions about who will own the result in three years. Getting that clear matters more than any feature comparison.

By Anthony PatersonPublished 25 September 20263 min read

On this page

  1. The platform is rarely the problem
  2. Legacy is the normal starting point
  3. Governance decides whether it sticks
  4. Make ownership part of the build
  5. Three questions before comparing vendors

Platform conversations usually start in the wrong place: a feature comparison, a vendor roadmap, a benchmark that will be irrelevant in eighteen months. The question that decides whether a platform decision was a good one is quieter. Who will own this, and can they sustain it, three years from now?

The platform is rarely the problem

The major cloud data platforms are all capable of doing the job. What separates a successful platform from an expensive one is whether the organisation has the operating model, the governance and the internal capability to run what gets built after the project team has gone. A technically excellent platform that one person understands isn’t a data capability. It’s a risk with good documentation.

Legacy is the normal starting point

Fragmented systems, on-premises constraints and security dependencies aren’t special cases. They’re the usual conditions, particularly in government and higher education. Waiting for a clean slate before modernising is how organisations end up running the same workarounds for another decade. The practical path is an architecture that works within today’s constraints while creating a deliberate route to something more sustainable, rather than a single large replacement that depends on everything going right at once.

Governance decides whether it sticks

Platforms that get adopted, and stay adopted, have clear ownership from the first deployment: who approves a new data source, who is accountable for a failed pipeline, who signs off before a model reaches production. Adding governance after a platform is live costs far more than designing it in, and its absence is usually why a promising platform drifts back to spreadsheets within two years.

Make ownership part of the build

The most reliable way to avoid dependency is to build the platform alongside the people who will run it. That means internal staff in the delivery team from the first sprint, code held in the organisation’s own repositories, and design decisions written down with the reasoning behind them. It takes slightly longer at the start. It saves years of expensive reliance on whoever happened to build the first version.

Three questions before comparing vendors

  1. What decisions does this platform need to support, and who makes them?
  2. Who will own and operate it once it’s built, and what will they need to learn?
  3. What does proportionate governance look like for this organisation, given its obligations?

The answers should shape the architecture, not the other way round. Once they’re clear, the technology choice is usually easier than expected.
