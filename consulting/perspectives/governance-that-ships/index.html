Governance that ships

Governance has a reputation for slowing delivery down. Done well, it does the opposite: it lets teams move quickly because the hard decisions were made once, clearly.

By Anthony PatersonPublished 25 September 20262 min read

On this page

  1. Evidence before design
  2. Design for the decision
  3. Targeted review, not blanket sign-off
  4. Ownership is a name, not a team
  5. Measure whether it’s working
  6. Four practices, not four documents

For many delivery teams, governance means a committee, a form and a wait. That reputation is earned, but it describes governance done badly. Good governance is what lets a team move with confidence, because ownership, risk and quality were settled once instead of being argued again on every release.

Evidence before design

Good governance starts before a dashboard or model exists, with a clear question, the data behind it and the context needed to interpret it honestly. Skipping this is how organisations end up with technically correct reports that answer the wrong question.

Design for the decision

Information design and accessibility aren’t a finishing step. An insight that’s accurate but hard to read gets misread or ignored, which is its own governance failure. Designing for the decision the audience needs to make is what turns data into something people act on.

Targeted review, not blanket sign-off

Assurance that means “everyone reviews everything” doesn’t scale and isn’t taken seriously. Review focused on what actually carries risk, with a clear record of who checked what, makes governance credible without making it a bottleneck.

Ownership is a name, not a team

“The data team owns it” isn’t an accountability model. Governance that lasts names a person or a clearly defined role against each material decision: who approves a new source, who owns data quality for a domain, who is accountable when something breaks. Ambiguity here is where governance quietly stops working.

Measure whether it’s working

Governance should be judged by what it enables, not by how many artefacts it produces. Useful signals include how long it takes to approve a new data source, how often reports are corrected after release, and whether people can say who owns the numbers they rely on. If those are improving, governance is doing its job.

Four practices, not four documents

Swarm’s framework moves through evidence, design, assurance and governance. None of them is optional, and none of them is a document that sits on a shelf. Each is a working practice a team can sustain.
