Health records

Organising health information for someone you care for

When you help someone else manage their health, you become the person who needs to know where everything is. A little structure makes appointments, emergencies and handovers far easier.

By Swarm LabsUpdated 25 September 20263 min read

General informationThis guide is general organisational information, not medical advice. Do not start, stop or change a medicine based on a record or this guide. Speak with your doctor, pharmacist or another qualified health professional. In an emergency, call 000.

Caring for a parent, partner, child or friend often means keeping track of medicines, appointments and contacts on top of everything else. This guide describes a simple structure you can set up once and keep up to date as things change.

Start with a one-page summary

Create a single page, on paper or on a phone, that someone could read in an emergency:

  • the person’s full name and date of birth
  • health conditions, as described by their doctors
  • current medicines and doses
  • allergies and reactions
  • their GP, regular pharmacy and specialists, with phone numbers
  • emergency contacts

Keep Medicare and concession card details somewhere secure rather than on a page that might be left lying around.

Keep the medicines list current

Update the list whenever a medicine starts, stops or changes. Ask the pharmacist to help check it’s complete, especially when several prescribers are involved. Many pharmacies can also pack medicines into dose administration aids, which can make daily routines simpler.

If the person takes several medicines, ask their GP whether a Home Medicines Review is appropriate. In this Medicare-funded service, an accredited pharmacist reviews all their medicines, usually at home, and reports back to the GP.

Track appointments in one place

Put every appointment in a calendar you both can see, and add a short note afterwards: what was discussed, what changed and what happens next. Over time, these notes become a useful history, especially when a new clinician asks what’s already been tried.

When things change quickly

If the person goes to hospital, take the one-page summary and the medicines list with you. Before they’re discharged, ask for a copy of the discharge summary and an updated medicines list, and check whether any medicines have been stopped, started or changed. Update your own records the same day, and let their GP and pharmacy know if they haven’t been told.

Share the load

If several family members help, agree who looks after what: one person for appointments and transport, another for medicines and the pharmacy, for example. A shared calendar and a single, current summary stop important information from living in one person’s head.

Understand consent and access

Health professionals generally need the person’s consent before sharing their information with you. Ask the person, where they’re able, to tell their GP and other providers that you’re involved in their care.

My Health Record allows people to give others access. A person can add you as a nominated representative so you can view or help manage their record. An authorised representative can act for someone who can’t manage their own record. The Australian Digital Health Agency explains how each works.

Protect the information

Health information is sensitive. Use a passcode on devices that hold it, share documents only with people who need them, and remove access when someone no longer needs it.

Look after yourself too

Caring is demanding work. Carer Gateway is a free Australian Government service offering counselling, peer support, coaching and practical help for carers. Call 1800 422 737 or visit the Carer Gateway website.
