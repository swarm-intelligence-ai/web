How to budget your first full-time wage

Your first proper pay can feel large until the annual bills, car costs and subscriptions arrive. This plan starts with the money that actually reaches your account.

General informationThis guide is general information, not personal financial advice. It does not consider your objectives, financial situation or needs. Consider whether it suits your circumstances, and seek licensed advice where you need it.

Start with your take-home pay, not the salary in your contract. The deposit in your bank account is the amount you can allocate. Superannuation matters, but it is not available for this week’s rent or next month’s registration.

Convert everything to one rhythm

If you are paid fortnightly, plan fortnightly. Convert monthly and annual costs into the same rhythm so they stop arriving as surprises.

  • For a monthly bill, multiply by 12 and divide by 26 to get the fortnightly amount.
  • For an annual bill, divide by 26.
  • Round up slightly so small price rises don’t break the plan.

ExampleA $1,300 annual insurance bill needs about $50 from each fortnightly pay. Move it to a separate bills account before you spend the rest.

Give the irregular bills a home

List registration, insurance, servicing, phone, subscriptions, medical costs and any regular contribution at home. Transfer their combined share on payday. A separate bills account makes the balance in your everyday account more honest: what’s left there is genuinely yours to spend.

Pick one useful buffer target

A first buffer doesn’t need to cover months of expenses. Start with an amount that could absorb a tyre, an insurance excess, an urgent trip or a lost shift. Once it exists, build toward a larger emergency fund at a pace you can sustain. Our guide to building an emergency fund covers the next step.

Separate goals from general saving

“Save more” is hard to act on. “Put $80 a fortnight toward a $2,000 car repair fund” has a number and a finish line. Keep one or two active goals at a time so progress stays visible.

Decide the fun money on purpose

A budget with no room for enjoyment rarely survives. Choose an amount you can spend without guilt once bills, essentials and goals are covered. If the number feels too tight, extend a goal’s timeline before borrowing from next fortnight.

Review after three real pay cycles

Your first plan is a forecast. Check it against three pay cycles, then adjust the amounts using what actually happened. Keep the structure and change the numbers.

A worked example

Here’s how one fortnightly plan might look for someone taking home $2,400. The numbers are illustrative. Yours will depend on where you live, how you get around and what you’re saving for.

Take-home pay
$2,400 a fortnight
Bills account
$380: phone, insurance, registration, servicing and subscriptions, averaged
Essentials
$1,080: rent share, groceries and transport
Goals
$300: car fund and a trip
Buffer
$140, until it reaches your first target
Fun money
$500, spent without guilt

Every dollar has a job before the fortnight starts. If something unexpected comes up, you can see exactly which category it has to come from.

Common traps in the first year

  • Lifestyle creep. Spending rises to match income without anyone deciding it should. Setting the fun money on purpose is the defence.
  • Forgotten annual costs. Registration, insurance renewals and memberships arrive once a year. Average them into your bills account from the start.
  • Subscriptions that multiply. Streaming, apps and memberships add up quietly. Review them every few months and cancel what you don’t use.
  • Buy now, pay later. Instalment plans are still commitments. If you use one, treat the repayments as a bill, not as fun money.

If your pay changes week to week

If you work casual or variable hours, plan around a low but typical pay rather than your best fortnight. When a bigger pay arrives, send the extra to your buffer or a goal instead of spending it straight away. Over time, the buffer smooths out the lean weeks.

A practical payday orderBills first, essential spending second, goals and buffer third, fun money last. Automate the transfers once the amounts have survived a few real weeks.

Tools that help

Most banks let you open extra accounts with no monthly fee and name them by purpose, which makes the bills-account approach easy to run. A budgeting app can help you see spending by category, but the structure above works just as well in a spreadsheet or a notebook. Choose whichever you’ll actually open each payday.
