A shared family calendar that people actually use

Most shared calendars fail quietly. Entries go stale, half the family stops checking, and it becomes one more place to look. A few simple rules keep it trustworthy.

A family calendar only works if everyone trusts it. The moment it’s out of date, people stop checking, and it stops being useful. These rules are about keeping it accurate with as little effort as possible.

Set it up in one evening

  1. Create the shared calendar and invite each adult, and older children if they have their own phones.
  2. Add the school term dates, pupil-free days and public holidays for the year.
  3. Add each recurring activity with an end date at the end of term, so finished activities don’t linger.
  4. Add the deadlines you already know about, each with an alert.
  5. Agree the naming pattern and colours described below.

One calendar of record

Pick one shared calendar that everyone in the household can see on their own phone. Most phone and email platforms include shared calendars at no extra cost. The choice of app matters much less than agreeing that this is the one place family commitments live.

Decide what goes in

A calendar is for things with a date and time: events, appointments and deadlines. It isn’t for storing whole notices, shopping lists or long instructions. Put the detail in a linked note or attach the original, and keep the calendar entry short.

Use a naming pattern

Start each entry with who it’s for, then what it is: “Sam: swimming lesson” or “Mia: excursion consent due”. A consistent pattern makes the calendar readable at a glance, even on a small screen.

Colour by person, or by calendar

Either give each person their own colour, or set up a separate calendar for each person inside the shared account. Separate calendars let people hide what isn’t relevant to them while keeping everything in one place.

Set alerts for deadlines, not just events

Events are rarely forgotten. Deadlines are. Give every deadline an alert two days before it falls due, and events an alert that allows for travel time. Turn off alerts that nobody acts on, so the useful ones still get attention.

Put a name against every action

If an entry needs someone to do something, like pay, sign, pack or drive, name that person in the entry or invite only them. Visibility isn’t ownership.

Older children and their own calendars

As children get older, it helps to give them their own calendar inside the shared account. They take responsibility for adding their own commitments, and adults can still see what’s coming up. It’s a useful step toward managing their own time.

Keep it tidy weekly

Use the same weekly check-in you use for school notices to correct times, delete cancelled items and add anything new. Ten minutes a week is enough to keep a calendar trustworthy.

Think about privacy

Family calendars can hold a lot about children: schools, locations and routines. Share the calendar only with the people who need it, and be careful when sharing it with wider groups such as sports teams or extended family.

Good to know

This guide is general information for organising family life. Adapt it to your household and your school’s own requirements.
