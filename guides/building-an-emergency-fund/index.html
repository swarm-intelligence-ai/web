Building an emergency fund

An emergency fund is money set aside for the costs you can’t plan for. Having one turns a car repair or a lost shift from a crisis into an inconvenience.

Everyone eventually faces an expense that won’t wait: a dental bill, a broken laptop you need for work, an urgent flight home. Without savings, those costs end up on a credit card or a buy now, pay later plan, and cost more than they should. An emergency fund is the simplest protection there is.

What it’s for, and what it isn’t

An emergency fund covers costs that are both unexpected and urgent. It isn’t for bills you can predict, like annual registration or insurance. Those belong in a bills account, set aside a little each payday. Keeping the two separate stops your emergency money quietly paying for things you knew were coming.

How much to aim for

Moneysmart suggests a good target is enough to cover three months of expenses. For someone just starting out, that can feel a long way off, so break it into stages:

  1. A first milestone of about $1,000. Enough to absorb most minor emergencies. Moneysmart notes that saving $20 a week gets you past $1,000 in a year.
  2. One month of essential expenses. Rent, groceries, transport, phone and minimum debt repayments.
  3. Three months of essential expenses. A cushion for bigger disruptions, like changing jobs.

To find your number, add up your essential monthly costs and multiply. Leave out the things you would cut in a real emergency.

Where to keep it

Keep your emergency fund in a separate savings account, away from your everyday spending account and not linked to a card. You want it to be available within a day, but not so convenient that it gets spent on a whim.

If you own a home with a mortgage offset account, Moneysmart notes that the offset account can act as your emergency fund, reducing interest while keeping the money accessible.

Choosing a savings account

When comparing savings accounts for an emergency fund, look beyond the headline rate:

  • Base rate versus bonus rate. Many accounts pay a higher rate only if you meet conditions each month, such as depositing a set amount or making no withdrawals. An emergency fund may need to be withdrawn from, so check what you’d earn if you missed the conditions.
  • Introductory rates. Check what the rate becomes once an introductory period ends.
  • Fees and access. Look for no monthly fees and transfers to your everyday account that arrive quickly.
  • Government protection. Deposits with Australian banks, building societies and credit unions are protected by the Financial Claims Scheme up to $250,000 per account holder, per institution.

Building it without noticing

Set up an automatic transfer from your pay account to your emergency fund on payday, so the money moves before you can spend it. Start with an amount you won’t miss and increase it when your pay rises. Put part of any windfall, such as a tax refund, straight into the fund.

Using it, then refilling it

Decide your rules in advance: it’s for unexpected and urgent costs only. When you do use it, don’t feel guilty. That’s what it’s for. Pause other savings goals if you need to and rebuild the fund first.

What if you have debt?

If you’re carrying high-interest debt, such as a credit card balance, it can make sense to keep a small emergency buffer while putting most of your spare money toward the debt. Without any buffer, the next unexpected bill tends to go straight back on the card. Moneysmart’s guidance on managing debt is a good next step if this applies to you.

Common mistakes

  • Keeping it in your everyday account. It blends in with spending money and slowly disappears.
  • Chasing the highest rate at the cost of access. Emergency money needs to be reachable within a day or two, so avoid locking it into a term deposit.
  • Using it for predictable costs. Christmas, birthdays and registration aren’t emergencies. Plan for them separately.
  • Waiting for the perfect amount to start. Twenty dollars a week is a real start.
